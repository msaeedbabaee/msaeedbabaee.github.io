When evaluating slope stability, geotechnical engineers frequently rely on two-dimensional (2D) plane-strain assumptions. While 2D limit equilibrium (LEM) and finite element (FEM) models provide computationally efficient estimates, they often obscure the three-dimensional reality of slope failure mechanisms.

\


In real-world excavations, road cuts, and open-pit mines, slopes are spatially bounded. The boundary constraints at the ends of an excavation create a phenomenon known as **End-Effects**—a key factor that frequently renders 2D analyses conservative and dictates true 3D slope behavior.

\


## 1. What are End-Effects?

In reality, no trench, excavation, or slope extends infinitely. When a longitudinal trench is excavated (e.g., for a road cut, pipeline, or foundation), its two longitudinal ends remain constrained by and connected to the adjacent intact, stable soil mass.

\


This boundary constraint creates a **hidden 3D resistance**—akin to side-wall friction, lateral restraint, and shear resistance along the side boundaries—that helps stabilize the sliding mass as a whole.

\


## 2. Comparing 2D and 3D Analyses from an End-Effect Perspective

### Two-Dimensional (2D) Model

* **Fundamental Assumption:** The cross-section extends infinitely along the longitudinal axis ($Z$-axis) under a plane-strain condition ($\epsilon\_z = 0$).


* **Limitation:** The 2D software ignores the closed ends of the trench, effectively assuming zero lateral shear resistance from side boundaries.


* **Outcome:** The calculated Factor of Safety ($\text\{FoS}$) is inherently **conservative (lower)** because the driving weight of the soil must be resisted solely by shear strength along the basal slip surface without side boundary contributions.



### Three-Dimensional (3D) Model

* **Realistic Assumption:** The trench or slope has a finite length ($L$).


* **Mechanism:** As sliding impends, the soil mass must shear not only along the base but also along both longitudinal ends (End-Resistance / Lateral Shear Resistance).


* **Outcome:** The 3D Factor of Safety ($\text\{FoS}\_\{3D}$) is **higher and more realistic**, yielding an economical design while accurately capturing the physical failure mechanism.



## 3. Influence of Trench Geometry: Length-to-Height Ratio ($L/H$)

A critical aspect of slope assessment is evaluating when 3D end-effects become significant enough to warrant 3D numerical modeling:

\


$$\text\{Geometry Ratio} = \frac\{\text\{Length of Trench } (L)}\{\text\{Height of Slope } (H)}$$

| **Geometry Ratio (L/H)**                  | **End-Effect Significance** | **Recommended Approach**                        | **Typical FoS Variance (FoS3D​ vs. FoS2D​)** |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **$L/H > 5$ (Long Trenches)**             | Negligible                  | 2D Plane-Strain is sufficient & efficient       | $+1\\% \text\{ to } +5\\%$                   |
| **$3 \le L/H \le 5$ (Medium Trenches)**   | Moderate                    | 2D for conservative design; 3D for optimization | $+5\\% \text\{ to } +15\\%$                  |
| **$L/H \< 3$ (Short Trenches / Corners)** | Dominant                    | 3D Modeling required                            | $+15\\% \text\{ to } +40\\%+$                |

* **Long Trenches ($L/H > 5$):** End-effects influence only a small percentage of the total sliding volume. The difference between $\text\{FoS}\_\{2D}$ and $\text\{FoS}\_\{3D}$ is minor, making a 2D plane-strain assumption acceptable.


* **Short Trenches ($L/H \< 3$):** End-effects govern overall mass stability. Here, 2D models become overly conservative, whereas 3D models demonstrate that the slope is substantially more stable due to end constraints.



## 4. Types of Failure Surfaces: 2D Extrusions vs. 3D Realities

The geometry and shape of the failure surface directly reflect how end-effects constrain soil movement.

\


### 1. 2D Failure Surfaces

* **Circular Slip Surface:** Common in homogeneous soils and soft clays (analyzed via Bishop, Spencer, or 2D FEM).


* **Planar / Wedge Slip Surface:** Common in slopes with continuous weak bedding planes parallel to the slope face.


* **Composite Surface:** Follows horizontal weak layers or rockhead interfaces.



### 2. 3D Failure Surfaces

* **Ellipsoidal / Spherical Surface:** The 3D equivalent of circular surfaces in homogeneous soils, forming a **closed bowl-shaped sliding mass** that tapers to zero at both ends of the excavation.


* **3D Tetrahedral / Wedge Surface:** Formed by intersecting structural discontinuities or at excavation corners.


* **Complex 3D Non-Circular Surfaces:** Identified using advanced 3D search algorithms (e.g., Cuckoo Search in LEM) or finite element strength reduction ($\phi/c$ reduction in PLAXIS 3D), tracking the true path of minimum resistance around localized obstacles.



## 5. Interaction with Localized Weak Lenses and Seams

The presence of a **weak soil lens or structural discontinuity** highlights another major divergence between 2D and 3D modeling:

\


* **In 2D Models:** A weak lens appearing in the cross-section is assumed to extend indefinitely across the entire project length. This forces the critical failure surface through the weak material over an infinite length, drastically underestimating overall stability.


* **In 3D Models:** If the weak lens has finite spatial dimensions, the 3D failure surface can either cut through it locally or bypass it altogether. The surrounding competent soil mobilizes additional shear strength, reflecting the true spatial variability of the site.



## 6. Visualizing End-Effects in PLAXIS 3D Outputs

When comparing 2D and 3D finite element outputs (e.g., PLAXIS 2D vs. PLAXIS 3D using $\phi/c$ reduction):

\


1. **Factor of Safety Output:**

   \


   $$\text\{FoS}\_\{2D} = 1.15 \quad \text\{vs.} \quad \text\{FoS}\_\{3D} = 1.35$$

   *(Demonstrating that an apparently marginal slope in 2D falls well within safe operational limits in 3D).*


2. **Plastic Points & Shear Strain Contours ($\gamma\_\{s}$):**

   \


   * **PLAXIS 2D:** Shows a continuous, uniform shear band extending across the entire basal profile.


   * **PLAXIS 3D:** Displays a distinct **closed "Bowl-Shaped" surface**. Incremental deviatoric strains peak in the center of the trench and gradually diminish toward intact soil at both boundaries.



## 7. Practical Engineering & Economic Implications

1. **Preventing Over-Engineering:** Relying exclusively on 2D models for slopes with low $L/H$ ratios often leads to unnecessary structural remediation (such as installing costly soil nails, ground anchors, or retaining walls) on slopes that are inherently stable due to 3D geometric confinement.


2. **Soil Arching & Stress Redistribution:** Near excavation corners or short trench ends, 3D stress redistribution causes soil arching. Active earth pressures are transferred away from the center toward rigid, unexcavated boundaries, further enhancing stability.


3. **Boundary Condition Sensitivity in Numerical Modeling:** When setting up 3D finite element meshes (e.g., PLAXIS 3D), the longitudinal domain length must extend far enough beyond the trench ($Z\_\{\text\{domain}} \ge 1.5 \times L\_\{\text\{trench}}$) to prevent artificial fixed boundary conditions from falsely inflating the calculated $\text\{FoS}\_\{3D}$.



## Conclusion

Understanding end-effects is essential for modern geotechnical practice. While 2D plane-strain analyses remain a valuable screening tool due to their simplicity, 3D analyses provide the true factor of safety, accurate failure surface geometry, and optimized engineering solutions for bounded slopes, trenches, and complex ground conditions.
