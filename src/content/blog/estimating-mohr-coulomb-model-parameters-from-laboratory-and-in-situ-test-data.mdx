Estimating Mohr-Coulomb Model Parameters from Laboratory and In-Situ Test Data: Standard Engineering Practice, Back-Analysis, and Modeling Pitfalls

Accurately determining Mohr-Coulomb parameters is a fundamental step in numerical modeling across general geotechnical software packages—such as **PLAXIS**, **FLAC / FLAC3D**, **GEO5**, **SIGMA/W**, **RS2 / RS3**, and **MIDAS GTS NX**. While advanced constitutive models capture stress-dependent stiffness and non-linear strain behavior, the **Mohr-Coulomb (MC)** linear elastic-perfectly plastic model remains the primary baseline across North American, European, Iranian, and international consulting practices due to its transparency, computational efficiency, and widely accepted parameter relationships.

\\

## 1. Applications, Advantages, and Limitations of the Mohr-Coulomb Model

### A) Typical Applications

* **Slope Stability & Landslides:** Calculating Factors of Safety (FoS) via shear strength reduction (c-phi reduction techniques).
* **Foundation Bearing Capacity:** Estimating ultimate bearing capacity for shallow strip, isolated, or raft foundations.
* **Retaining Structures & Excavation Support:** Estimating active/passive earth pressures and global wall stability for anchored or sheet pile walls.
* **Tunnelling & Caverns:** Preliminary stability assessment and plastic zone estimation in rock masses or stiff clays.

### B) Key Advantages

* **Simplicity & Minimum Parameters:** Requires only 5 physical parameters that are easily understood and obtainable from standard site investigations.
* **High Computational Efficiency:** Linear elasticity combined with a fixed yield surface ensures fast convergence in FEA/FDA solvers.
* **Direct Alignment with Standard Codes:** Parameter inputs align directly with traditional limit equilibrium equations and design codes (AASHTO, Eurocode 7, etc.).

### C) Limitations

* **Constant Elastic Modulus:** Ignores stress-dependent stiffness (stiffness does not increase with depth/confining stress automatically).
* **Linear Elasticity Before Yield:** Overestimates soil stiffness at larger strains and fails to model small-strain stiffness nonlinearity.
* **Symmetric Unloading/Reloading:** Uses the same modulus $E$ for primary loading and unloading, leading to unrealistically large bottom heave predictions in deep excavations.
* **No Strain Hardening/Softening:** Assumes ideal plasticity, ignoring peak-to-residual strength drops in dense sands or overconsolidated clays.

## 2. Primary Mohr-Coulomb Model Parameters

Regardless of the software package used, the Mohr-Coulomb model relies on five core parameters:

\\

| **Parameter**       | **Symbol**    | **Standard Units**                             | **Engineering Role**                                       |
| ------------------- | ------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Young's Modulus** | $E$           | $\text\{kPa}$, $\text\{MPa}$, or $\text\{ksf}$ | Governs elastic stiffness and deformation response         |
| **Poisson's Ratio** | $\nu$         | dimensionless                                  | Controls lateral expansion and elastic volumetric response |
| **Cohesion**        | $c'$ / $s\_u$ | $\text\{kPa}$ or $\text\{psf}$                 | Baseline shear strength at zero normal stress              |
| **Friction Angle**  | $\phi'$       | degrees ($^\circ$)                             | Confining stress-dependent shear strength gain             |
| **Dilatancy Angle** | $\psi$        | degrees ($^\circ$)                             | Controls plastic volumetric strain during shear            |

## 3. Laboratory Testing Procedures, Data Recording, and Curve Interpretation

Laboratory testing—primarily **Triaxial Compression (CD/CU/UU)** and **Direct Shear (DS)** tests—provides the primary physical basis for parameter selection.

\\

### A) Triaxial Compression Test (CD / CU)

#### 1. Testing Procedure & Data Recording

* **Specimen Preparation:** A cylindrical soil sample (typically $38 \text\{ mm}$ or $50 \text\{ mm}$ diameter with a 2:1 height-to-diameter ratio) is trimmed and saturated inside a triaxial cell.
* **Consolidation Phase:** Isotropic cell pressure ($\sigma\_3$) is applied. In a Consolidated Drained (CD) or Consolidated Undrained (CU) test, drainage is allowed until consolidation completes under $\sigma\_3$.
* **Shearing Phase:** Axial force ($F$) is applied continuously via a loading ram at a controlled displacement rate while cell pressure ($\sigma\_3$) is kept constant.
* **Recorded Variables:**

  \\

  * Axial displacement ($\Delta L$) $\rightarrow$ Axial Strain: $\varepsilon\_a = \frac\{\Delta L}\{L\_0}$
  * Axial force ($F$) $\rightarrow$ Deviator Stress: $q = (\sigma\_1 - \sigma\_3) = \frac\{F}\{A\_c}$ *(where $A\_c$ is the corrected cross-sectional area)*
  * Volumetric change ($\Delta V$) \[CD test] $\rightarrow$ Volumetric Strain: $\varepsilon\_v = \frac\{\Delta V}\{V\_0}$
  * Excess pore water pressure ($\Delta u$) \[CU test]

#### 2. Plotted Curves & Graphical Interpretations

* **Deviator Stress ($q$) vs. Axial Strain ($\varepsilon\_a$):** Plotted for multiple confining pressures ($\sigma\_\{3a}, \sigma\_\{3b}, \sigma\_\{3c}$) to evaluate non-linear stiffness and peak strength $q\_\{\text\{max}}$.
* **Mohr Circles & Failure Envelope:** Normal effective stress $\sigma'$ vs. Shear stress $\tau$ at peak failure.

#### 3. Parameter Extraction Equations

* **Secant Young's Modulus ($E\_\{50}$):**

  Since the Mohr-Coulomb model uses a single linear modulus, standard practice extracts the secant modulus at 50% peak deviator stress:

  \\

  $$E\_\{50} = \frac\{0.5 \cdot q\_\{\text\{max}}}\{\varepsilon\_\{50}}$$

  *(where $q\_\{\text\{max}} = \sigma\_\{1,f} - \sigma\_3$, and $\varepsilon\_\{50}$ is the strain at $0.5 \cdot q\_\{\text\{max}}$).*
* **Effective Cohesion ($c'$) and Friction Angle ($\phi'$):**

  \\

  1. Construct effective stress Mohr circles at failure using principal stresses:

     \\

     $$\sigma'\_\{1,f} = \sigma\_\{1,f} - u\_f, \quad \sigma'\_\{3,f} = \sigma\_\{3,f} - u\_f$$
  2. Draw the best-fit linear envelope tangent to the circles:

     \\

     $$\tau\_f = c' + \sigma' \tan(\phi')$$
  3. *Alternative ($p'-q$ Space Formulation):* Plot $p' = \frac\{\sigma'\_1 + \sigma'\_3}\{2}$ against $q = \sigma'\_1 - \sigma'\_3$ at failure. The linear fit yields slope $M$ and intercept $d$, converted via:

     \\

     $$\sin(\phi') = \frac\{M}\{2 + M}, \quad c' = d \cdot \frac\{1 - \sin(\phi')}\{2 \cdot \cos(\phi')}$$
* **Dilatancy Angle ($\psi$):**

  Calculated from the maximum rate of volume expansion ($\varepsilon\_v$) relative to axial strain ($\varepsilon\_a$) during shear:

  \\

  $$\sin(\psi) = \frac\{-\frac\{d\varepsilon\_v}\{d\varepsilon\_a}}\{2 - \frac\{d\varepsilon\_v}\{d\varepsilon\_a}}$$

### B) Direct Shear Test (DS)

#### 1. Procedure & Data Extraction

1. Soil is placed in a split shear box and consolidated under normal force $N$ ($\sigma = N / A$).
2. The box is sheared horizontally at a constant speed, recording shear force $T$ ($\tau = T / A$) and vertical displacement.
3. Peak shear stress ($\tau\_f$) is plotted against applied normal stress ($\sigma'$).
4. The linear envelope slope yields $\tan(\phi')$ and the vertical intercept yields $c'$.

### C) Soil-Structure Interface Parameter ($R\_\{\text\{inter}}$)

In software like PLAXIS, RS2, or MIDAS GTS NX, structural elements (retaining walls, piles, geotextiles) require interface properties to simulate soil-structure interaction. The interface reduction factor $R\_\{\text\{inter}}$ scales the soil strength parameters:

\\

$$c\_\{\text\{interface}} = R\_\{\text\{inter}} \cdot c\_\{\text\{soil}}$$

$$\tan(\phi\_\{\text\{interface}}) = R\_\{\text\{inter}} \cdot \tan(\phi\_\{\text\{soil}})$$

* **Concrete to Soil:** $R\_\{\text\{inter}} \approx 0.65 - 0.70$
* **Steel Sheet Piles to Sand:** $R\_\{\text\{inter}} \approx 0.50 - 0.60$
* **Geotextile / Geogrid to Soil:** $R\_\{\text\{inter}} \approx 0.80 - 0.90$

## 4. In-Situ Field Test Correlations

When undisturbed laboratory samples are difficult to obtain, field correlations are widely utilized across North American, European, and Middle Eastern practices.

\\

### A) Standard Penetration Test (SPT)

Using energy- and overburden-corrected blow counts ($N\_\{60}$ or $(N\_1)\_\{60}$):

\\

* **Friction Angle ($\phi'$):**

* **Young's Modulus ($E$):**

  \\

  * Normally consolidated sands: $E \text\{ (MPa)} = 0.75 \cdot N\_\{60} \quad \text\{or} \quad E \text\{ (ksf)} = 15 \cdot N\_\{60}$
  * Dense sands / gravels: $E \text\{ (MPa)} = (1.0 \text\{ to } 1.2) \cdot N\_\{60}$

### B) Cone Penetration Test (CPT)

* **Friction Angle ($\phi'$):** $\phi' = \arctan \left\[ 0.1 + 0.38 \cdot \log\left(\frac\{q\_c}\{\sigma'\_\{v0}}\right) \right]$
* **Undrained Shear Strength ($s\_u$):** $s\_u = \frac\{q\_c - \sigma\_\{v0}}\{N\_\{kt}} \quad (N\_\{kt} = 12 - 18)$
* **Young's Modulus ($E$):** $E = 2.5 \cdot q\_c \quad \text\{(Sands)}, \quad E = (3 \text\{ to } 8) \cdot q\_c \quad \text\{(Clays)}$

## 6. Worked Numerical Example (Laboratory Extraction)

### Scenario:

A Drained Triaxial Test (CD) on a dense sand sample ($\sigma\_3' = 100 \text\{ kPa}$) yields:

\\

* Peak deviator stress: $q\_\{\text\{max}} = 350 \text\{ kPa}$ at axial strain $\varepsilon\_a = 4.2\\%$.
* At $50\\%$ peak deviator stress ($q\_\{50} = 175 \text\{ kPa}$), axial strain is $\varepsilon\_\{50} = 0.50\\%$.
* Maximum volumetric contraction rate relative to axial strain gives $\frac\{d\varepsilon\_v}\{d\varepsilon\_a} = -0.18$.

### Calculation Steps:

1. **Secant Modulus ($E\_\{50}$):**

   \\

   $$E\_\{50} = \frac\{175 \text\{ kPa}}\{0.0050} = 35,000 \text\{ kPa} = 35 \text\{ MPa}$$
2. **Major Principal Stress at Failure ($\sigma\_\{1,f}'$):**

   \\

   $$\sigma\_\{1,f}' = q\_\{\text\{max}} + \sigma\_3' = 350 + 100 = 450 \text\{ kPa}$$
3. **Friction Angle $\phi'$ (Assuming $c' \approx 1.0 \text\{ kPa}$ for numerical stability):**

   \\

   $$\sin(\phi') = \frac\{\sigma\_\{1,f}' - \sigma\_\{3,f}'}\{\sigma\_\{1,f}' + \sigma\_\{3,f}'} = \frac\{450 - 100}\{450 + 100} = \frac\{350}\{550} = 0.636 \implies \phi' = 39.5^\circ$$
4. **Dilatancy Angle ($\psi$):**

   \\

   $$\sin(\psi) = \frac\{-(-0.18)}\{2 - (-0.18)} = \frac\{0.18}\{2.18} = 0.0825 \implies \psi \approx 4.7^\circ \quad (\text\{or via Bolton: } \psi \approx 39.5^\circ - 30^\circ = 9.5^\circ)$$

## 7. Numerical Calibration, Optimization, and Inverse Analysis

Due to boundary friction and non-uniform stress states in real physical testing, directly inputting raw laboratory values into FEA/FDA software can cause discrepancies. Practicing numerical modelers perform **Inverse Analysis / Digital Twin Calibration** using drivers like PLAXIS SoilTest, FLAC Single Element, or Python optimization scripts.

## 8. Common Pitfalls and Modeling Errors

1. **Setting Cohesion to Zero ($c' = 0$):**

   \\

   * *Issue:* Purely frictional materials with $c' = 0$ cause division-by-zero or matrix singularity issues at unconfined ground surface elements in FEA codes (PLAXIS / RS2).
   * *Fix:* Always apply a small artificial cohesion ($c' = 1 - 2 \text\{ kPa}$ or $20 - 40 \text\{ psf}$).
2. **Inputting $\nu = 0.50$ for Undrained Analysis:**

   \\

   * *Issue:* Entering $\nu = 0.50$ in effective stress coupled undrained formulations (PLAXIS *Undrained A*) leads to an infinite bulk modulus ($K \rightarrow \infty$) and immediate numerical crash.
   * *Fix:* Input drained Poisson's ratio ($\nu \approx 0.30$). The software automatically computes pore water stiffness through the fluid bulk modulus.
3. **Using Initial Tangent Modulus Instead of $E\_\{50}$:**

   \\

   * *Issue:* Using the initial small-strain slope ($E\_0$) severely overestimates soil stiffness, resulting in unconservatively small settlement predictions.
4. **Ignoring Operational Strain Level:**

   \\

   * *Issue:* Applying a single $E\_\{50}$ derived at high failure strains to model retaining wall deflections (which occur at small strain levels) underpredicts soil stiffness.

## 9. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1: Why do we use $E\_\{50}$ instead of initial elastic modulus $E\_\{\text\{init}}$ in Mohr-Coulomb models?**

*Answer:* Because the Mohr-Coulomb model is elastic-perfectly plastic, its elastic slope is constant up to failure. Using $E\_\{\text\{init}}$ matches the very beginning of loading but drastically overestimates stiffness as the soil approaches yield. $E\_\{50}$ represents an optimal secant average over the typical working stress range ($0 - 50\\%$ peak load).

\\

**Q2: How do I handle soil stiffness increasing with depth using Mohr-Coulomb?**

*Answer:* Standard Mohr-Coulomb uses a constant $E$. To model depth dependency in software like PLAXIS or RS2, you must either subdivide the soil stratum into multiple sub-layers with increasing $E\_i$ values or use the $E\_\{\text\{increment}}$ parameter ($E(y) = E\_\{\text\{ref}} + E\_\{\text\{inc}} \cdot (y\_\{\text\{ref}} - y)$).

\\

**Q3: When should I upgrade from Mohr-Coulomb to advanced models like Hardening Soil (HS)?**

*Answer:* Upgrade to **Hardening Soil (HS)** or **HS-Small** for deep excavations, tunneling under sensitive structures, or complex unloading scenarios where stress-dependent stiffness, small-strain non-linearity, and distinct unloading-reloading moduli ($E\_\{\text\{ur}}$) significantly control structural deflections.
